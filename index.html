Haystack - an LLM prompt generator for gene analysis

This tool checks a 23andMe or Ancestry.com raw data file against a preset list of genetic mutations that have mental health implications. It outputs a prompt that can be pasted into an LLM of your choice to do further analysis. This tool does not require internet access and your data is processed locally on your own machine. Of course, once you take the output and place it into an LLM, the data leaves your machine at that point. See the FAQ below for more info.

FAQ

What is the point of uploading both data files?

The 23andMe and Ancestry tests do not read the entire genome, only a portion of it. And each one reads a slightly different portion. There is a significant amount of overlap between the tests, but each one does read some SNPs that the other does not. So by uploading both, you get the most comprehensive picture possible without resorting to full genome sequencing. Not only that, but uploading both files also lets you double-check the accuracy of the data. Any mismatches found between the two files will be output with a slash in the tool output (like "rs12345=CT/TT"), indicating that there was disagreement between the data files on that particular SNP.

Should I trust what the LLM tells me about my genes?

No, probably not. Every single part of this process is extremely prone to errors. There could be errors in the raw data file that you got from 23andMe or Ancestry. They are doing genetics very inexpensively and in a low-stakes arena (ie, if they tell you that you are Italian rather than Irish, nobody dies), so errors are possible. It is amazing they can do gene analysis at all at the prices they sell the kits at; perfection should not be expected at this price. Then you are putting that potentially flawed file into this tool that was completely vibe coded, and is using a reference list of SNPs that was assembled by hand by an amateur. This tool probably will introduce more errors. And then of course, the LLM itself is very prone to be short-sighted or hallucinate entirely. Ultimately, you are probably about as likely to get good advice from consulting tarot cards as you are from putting the output of this tool into an LLM. Be very skeptical.

Should I take health advice from an LLM?

No, don't do that. The dangerous thing about LLMs is that their output often sounds very plausible even as it wanders wildly between accurate and completely false, and without expertise or background in the field, it is very hard to tell the true parts of the output apart from the false. It would be very unwise to take health advice from an LLM.

Should I put my genetic information into an LLM at all?

No, probably not. The privacy implications of putting your genetic information into an LLM are not very good.

What LLM should I use?

You will most likely want to use one that is paid/pro; the free ones just don't seem to have enough memory or thinking time to do a good job. They get really confused and start to hallucinate. One dead giveaway that you do not have a powerful enough LLM is if it begins to respond to the prompt immediately. Realistically this is a complicated enough prompt that it should take a little bit of time to research and respond to; if the LLM begins typing back immediately it is most likely making things up. Another good way to detect hallucination is to familiarize yourself with a few of your variants in advance so you have some pre-existing knowledge; this way you can spot check what the LLM is telling you against what you already know to be true. Low powered LLMs will output blatantly false information even about common variants and get very confused about direction of effect. As you move up into mid-tier LLMs you see them taking more time to respond and doing a much better job. The main things I notice when working with mid-tier LLMs are that they tend to resort to very broad generalizations and they tend to speak like a podcaster or functional medicine person, in very broad "storytelling" terms. They are usually telling the truth and getting things broadly right, but the entire response reads like a skimming overview with no real depth. They also will engage with more fringe/pseudoscientific theories. At the high end, you start to get very detailed, organized, granular responses and thoughtful consideration of whole-system interactions. Another good tell that differentiates mid-tier from high-tier LLMs: mid-tier LLMs engage with the project enthusiastically while high-tier LLMs are skeptical, pointing out the limitations of this approach, and sometimes have to be badgered into doing the analysis at all. The high-tier LLMs will also discover popular pseudoscientific theories about genes but won't believe them and will note why they don't believe them. Overall I would say you want to look for thinking time and token burn; if you want a good result you should see the LLM doing extended thinking and most likely it will burn a good chunk of tokens. Out of the LLMs I have tried I feel like Claude does the best and Gemini is also okay (again, paid/pro versions are a must).

How can I learn more?

If you find all of this interesting and would like to investigate genes in a better way, I would recommend checking out GeneticLifeHacks.com. She writes interesting articles, sticks close to the science, does not shill supplements, and her site processes your data locally similar to this tool (your genetic data is never uploaded to a server). I am a huge fan of the GeneticLifeHacks site and do not think there is a better tool available for the person who is curious about genetics. This little tool (and the LLM-powered analysis it enables) are a botch job by comparison. Another somewhat decent thing to check out is StrateGene. I am not a huge fan of the "clean vs dirty" distinction they use, I feel like it oversimplifies things to the point of meaninglessness. And they also sell supplements which I find suspicious. But the StrateGene report itself is pretty cool and contains some nice diagrams and useful info. FoundMyFitness is not bad either.

What are some good follow-up questions I can ask the LLM?

Whatever you are curious about. I often ask how a specific drug or supplement might affect me, given my genes. It is also interesting to ask about what lifestyle interventions might be the most effective. You can ask about specific symptoms you are experiencing and see which genes are most likely causing them. The initial overview that the LLM returns is often very broad and non-specific, so you really do need to follow up with more specific questions to get any useful info.
